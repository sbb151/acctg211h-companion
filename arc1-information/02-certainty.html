2.1 Remove one difficulty before adding another

What would valuation and accounting look like if everyone knew future cash flows and the interest rate? For this chapter, assume there are no taxes or transaction costs and investors can borrow or lend at the same rate. Our small enterprise is financed entirely by owners. These are model assumptions, not descriptions of real businesses or requirements of GAAP.

A number can change because time passes, even when nobody learns anything. Income can be positive without being surprising. Cash can be received without all of it being income. Scott develops this ideal-conditions approach (Scott and O’Brien 2020, chap. 2); the numerical cases here are teaching examples.

2.2 A payment in the future

Calculator warm-up. A 10% annual return turns 100 into 100 × 1.10 = 110 after one year and 110 × 1.10 = 121 after two. To work backward from 121, divide by 1.10 twice. Writing \((1.10)^2\) means \(1.10\times1.10\), not \(1.10\times2\). Always count the years between the valuation date and the payment date.

Suppose an investment pays $1,000 exactly two years from now. The annual interest rate is 5%. Investing $100 today gives you $105 after one year and $110.25 after two. Interest in the second year is earned on the accumulated amount.

Work backward to find the amount today that grows to $1,000:

\[ PV_0=\frac{1{,}000}{(1.05)^2}=907.03. \]

Present value is the value at a specified date of a future cash flow, calculated using a discount rate. The subscript 0 identifies today. One year from now the claim is worth

\[ PV_1=\frac{1{,}000}{1.05}=952.38. \]

Date 0: acquisition 1: one year later 2: payment
Value of remaining claim $907.03 $952.38 $0 after collection
Cash received — $0 $1,000

The claim gains $45.35 in Year 1 without a cash receipt. That is accretion of the discount: the payment is one year closer. It equals 5% of opening value, subject to rounding. At maturity, the $1,000 receipt includes recovery of the investment, not just the final year’s income.

Why is present value the price in this model? If an identical certain payment could be obtained more cheaply by lending, buyers would choose lending. If the claim offered a better return than the borrowing cost, investors could borrow to acquire it. Under our trading assumptions, those opportunities prevent different prices for identical payments. This is a no-arbitrage argument; it depends on the relevant trades being possible.

2.3 The Campus Curb model: one operating asset

A delivery business uses up productive resources. We create a simplified Campus Curb model enterprise, separate from the fuller operating-company case in Chapter 5.

It owns one delivery asset. Net operating cash receipts, after cash operating costs, will be $660 at the end of each of two years. The asset has no resale value after Year 2. The annual interest rate is 10%. All facts are publicly known. The enterprise buys the asset at its present value and immediately distributes each year’s $660 to owners. There are no further contributions, borrowing, or other activities.

\[ V_0=\frac{660}{1.10}+\frac{660}{(1.10)^2} =600+545.45=1{,}145.45. \tag{2.1}\]

Owners contribute $1,145.45 cash; the enterprise exchanges it for the asset. Immediately afterward:

Opening balance sheet Assets Liabilities and equity
Delivery asset $1,145.45 —
Liabilities — $0.00
Contributed equity — $1,145.45
Total $1,145.45 $1,145.45

The contribution is financing, not income. Purchasing the asset exchanges one resource for another. It does not create an immediate gain or loss in this benchmark.

2.4 The first year: cash, income, and capital recovery

At the end of Year 1, only the second $660 receipt remains. Its present value is $600. The delivery asset’s value falls from $1,145.45 to $600 as the first year’s services are used.

Economic depreciation is this decline in the productive asset’s value in our certainty example:

\[ 1{,}145.45-600=545.45. \]

Economic income is net cash generated less the reduction in asset value:

\[ I_1=660-545.45=114.55. \]

Equivalently, the owner’s economic income is the distribution plus the change in the remaining investment’s value:

\[ I_1=D_1+V_1-V_0=660+600-1{,}145.45=114.55. \tag{2.2}\]

Here \(D_t\) is the distribution and \(V_t\) is enterprise value after that distribution. There are no new contributions in this formula. A contribution must be excluded from income, just as we excluded the initial contribution.

Keep dates and periods separate. Asset value and equity describe what remains at a date; receipts, distributions, and income describe what happens during a period. In this all-equity model, enterprise value equals the value of all remaining assets, including any retained cash. It equals the delivery asset alone only when there is no other asset. We are using present values here; a later accounting carrying amount need not equal economic value.

Of the $660 received, $114.55 is a return on the investment and the remainder is capital recovery. Income is also 10% of opening value: $1,145.45 × 10% = $114.55.

A distribution is not an expense

Generating income and distributing cash are different events. If the enterprise instead kept the $660 at year-end, it would have $660 cash plus the $600 asset. Total value would be $1,260, with the same $114.55 income. A distribution reduces cash and equity; it does not reduce income.

Try a partial distribution. Suppose Campus Curb distributes only $400 of Year 1’s receipt and keeps the rest as cash at the reporting instant. Find closing cash, total assets, equity, and income before opening the answer. Everything else is unchanged.

Check the partial-distribution case

Cash is $260. The delivery asset is still worth $600, so total assets and equity are $860. Income is $400 + $860 − $1,145.45 = $114.55. Counting the full receipt as a distribution would count the retained $260 twice. Changing the payout alone has not changed this year’s income; future returns on retained cash are outside this reporting instant.

2.6 Complete the enterprise’s life

In Year 2 the asset produces its final $660 and has no remaining value. Economic depreciation is $600, income is $60, and the distribution is $660.

Item Year 1 Year 2 Lifetime total
Cash generated $660.00 $660.00 $1,320.00
Economic depreciation 545.45 600.00 1,145.45
Economic income 114.55 60.00 174.55
Closing asset after distribution 600.00 0.00 —

Lifetime income is receipts less the initial investment: $1,320 − $1,145.45 = $174.55. Both annual incomes were knowable at acquisition. The income statements record performance but add no new valuation information to what everyone already knew.

Records still document ownership claims and scheduled payments. It is the surprise and forecasting roles of income that disappear under these strong assumptions.

2.7 Change the convention, not the business

Keep every cash flow unchanged, but allocate acquisition cost equally over two years. Straight-line depreciation, with zero residual value, is

\[ \frac{1{,}145.45}{2}\approx572.73\text{ per year}. \]

Accounting income becomes approximately $660 − $572.73 = $87.27 each year. Use unrounded calculations and adjust the final cent when presenting a complete rounded schedule.

Convention Year 1 income Year 1 closing asset Year 2 income
Present-value model $114.55 $600.00 $60.00
Equal allocation of acquisition cost About $87.27 About $572.73 About $87.27

The convention changes reported income timing and carrying amounts. It does not change customer payments, productivity, or economic value in this example. Both conventions recognize the same lifetime cost.

Later we ask whether a measure helps users predict cash flows, evaluate management, compare firms, or verify reports. Selecting the measure with the largest current profit is not a sufficient answer.

2.9 Week 2 drills: certainty and linked statements

Work individually with a calculator, check the key, and correct errors. Submit before Day 4; these drills are practice after Day 3’s demonstration. Graded for completion. Do not use generative AI.

  1. A claim pays $121 in two years. At 10%, calculate its value today, its value one year later before any payment, and first-year income.
  2. An all-equity model enterprise costs its present value and distributes certain net receipts of $110 at the end of Year 1 and $121 at the end of Year 2. The rate is 10%, terminal value is zero, and there are no other transactions. Calculate opening value, Year 1 closing value after distribution, and each year’s economic income.
  3. In Problem 2, keep the Year 1 receipt as cash at year-end instead of distributing it. Give closing cash, the productive asset’s value, total equity, and Year 1 income. Do not project a second-year return on the retained cash.
  4. Allocate Problem 2’s acquisition cost evenly over its two years instead. Calculate annual depreciation and each year’s accounting income. Does this choice change the given cash flows or initial economic value?
  5. A new organization receives $50 from its owners and borrows $30. It has done nothing else. Give cash, liabilities, equity, and income. Explain why an $80 cash inflow is not $80 of revenue.
Drill answers
  1. Present value = 121/1.10² = $100. Year 1 value = 121/1.10 = $110. Income = $10.
  2. Opening value = 110/1.10 + 121/1.10² = $200. Closing Year 1 value = $110. Year 1 income = 110 + 110 − 200 = $20. Year 2 income = 121 − 110 = $11. Lifetime income is $31.
  3. Cash $110, productive asset $110, equity $220, income $20. No distribution occurred; retaining cash does not erase income.
  4. Annual depreciation $100. Income is $10 and $21. Lifetime income remains $31; given cash flows and initial value are unchanged.
  5. Cash $80, liabilities $30, equity $50, income $0. Contributions and borrowing are financing transactions.

2.10 Optional extension: residual income

If owners require return \(r\) on beginning book equity \(B_{t-1}\), define residual income as accounting income after a charge for capital:

\[ RI_t=NI_t-rB_{t-1}. \]

Under our present-value convention, income equals the required return and residual income is zero. Under equal cost allocation, first-year residual income is approximately −$27.27 and second-year residual income is $30. Their discounted sum is zero. Book equity plus discounted residual income gives the same initial valuation.

More generally, under the appropriate clean-surplus relation between income, equity, and net owner distributions, and a terminal condition, value can be expressed using book equity and discounted expected future residual income. The formula \(V=B+RI/r\) is a constant-perpetuity special case, not a general rule for valuing firms.
