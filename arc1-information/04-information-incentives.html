4  Information, Credibility, and Incentives

4.1 The report does not reveal everything

In Chapter 3, everyone learned the demand state perfectly at the end of Year 1. Now change that assumption. The true state remains unknown until Year 2, but a diagnostic report arrives at the end of Year 1. Keep the same cash flows: $660 in Year 1 and either $880 or $440 in Year 2, with equal prior probabilities. Retain risk neutrality and the 10% rate.

The report is favorable 80% of the time when demand is high. It is favorable 20% of the time when demand is low. These error rates are known to everyone. The report may be useful without being perfect.

4.2 Update with counts before using a formula

Imagine 100 equally likely cases generated by this model:

True state Favorable report Unfavorable report Total
High demand 40 10 50
Low demand 10 40 50
Total 50 50 100

Before receiving a report, 50 of 100 cases are high: the prior probability is 50%. After a favorable report, focus on the favorable column. Forty of its 50 cases are high, so the posterior probability is 80%. After an unfavorable report, only 10 of 50 cases are high, so it is 20%.

This is Bayesian updating: combine the starting distribution with the information conveyed by the observation. For the favorable report,

\[ P(H\mid F)=\frac{P(F\mid H)P(H)}{P(F\mid H)P(H)+P(F\mid L)P(L)} =\frac{0.8(0.5)}{0.8(0.5)+0.2(0.5)}=0.8. \]

The posterior equals the report’s 80% high-state detection rate here because the prior is 50% and the error rates are symmetric. It does not generally equal that rate. If high demand were rare, a favorable report could still leave substantial doubt.

From a report to value and income

After a favorable report, expected Year 2 cash is 0.8($880) + 0.2($440) = $792. After an unfavorable report, it is $528. Discount one year:

End of Year 1 Favorable report Unfavorable report
Probability of high demand 80% 20%
Expected Year 2 cash $792 $528
Remaining asset value $720 $480
Revision from prior expected value of $600 +$120 −$120
Year 1 economic income $234.55 −$5.45

Compute income as the $660 distribution plus the new remaining value less $1,145.45 opening value. Closing cash and liabilities are zero; equity equals the remaining asset, $720 or $480. The noisy report moves value less than perfect state revelation did because some uncertainty remains.

This is a further version of the same model, not an additional report after the state was already revealed. Once users know the state perfectly, a noisy signal of that same state adds no information.

4.3 How much is information worth for a decision?

Consider a separate prospective investment decision at the end of Year 1. An investor can pay $700 now for a claim to the same kind of Year 2 payoff. The investor has not bought it yet and can decline. This choice is distinct from valuing the claim already owned by the model enterprise.

Without a report, the claim is worth $600, so the investor declines. With the noisy report, she buys only after a favorable result: $720 value minus $700 price gives $20 of expected net benefit. A favorable report occurs with probability 0.5, so the expected benefit of having the report before paying its fee is $10. An $8 fee paid at that decision date leaves $2 net benefit.

With perfect information she would buy only in the high state, when value is $800. The expected benefit would be 0.5($800 − $700) = $50. This is an upper comparison for the information choices in this particular decision, not a general market price for perfect information.

Notice why the report helps: the investor changes her action. More precise information need not change every decision. If the price were low enough that she would buy after either report, this report would have no gross decision value in the simplified setting, even though it changes her valuation.

4.4 Who produces the report, and why believe it?

So far the diagnostic arrives automatically with known error rates. Real reports are produced by people. Suppose the founders observe the diagnostic privately. Outsiders now face an information asymmetry, and the founders may prefer a favorable interpretation.

Several mechanisms need to be distinguished:

Mechanism What carries information? Example
Routine accounting recognition A transaction, obligation, or use of resources Interest accumulated under a known loan contract.
Estimation A model applied to incomplete evidence An estimate of uncollectible customer balances.
Verifiable disclosure Truthful evidence that can be checked if released A signed customer contract shown to an investor.
Costly signaling An observable action that different types have different incentives to take A warranty that is more expensive for sellers of unreliable scooters.
Misstatement A depiction inconsistent with the relevant facts or requirements Omitting interest merely because no bill has arrived.

A useful number need not be a costly signal. A cost by itself does not make a statement true. Equally, an estimate that later differs from the realization need not have been a misstatement.

Why silence can be informative

If an informed seller can disclose a value truthfully, verifiably, and without cost, favorable types want to distinguish themselves from less favorable ones. As they disclose, the remaining silent pool becomes less attractive. This is the intuition behind disclosure unraveling (Grossman 1981; Milgrom 1981).

Real disclosure can be incomplete because releasing information has costs or because investors do not know whether a manager possesses information (Verrecchia 1983; Dye 1985). Silence can then reflect bad news, proprietary concerns, or a lack of information. It should not automatically be read as proof of fraud or as proof that the firm is the worst type.

What makes a reporting process credible?

For Campus Curb, compare the founders’ unsupported sales forecast with a report that identifies the underlying orders, distinguishes cancellable orders, explains collection assumptions, and is checked by an independent party. The latter gives users more ways to evaluate the claim.

Internal controls can separate authorization, custody, and recordkeeping; reconciliations can expose differences; outside confirmation can test whether a balance exists. These mechanisms have costs and do not remove all judgment. Their purpose is to make relevant claims more dependable and errors more likely to be detected.

4.5 Valuation and stewardship ask different questions

An investor wants to know what cash flows to expect. A compensation committee also wants to know what performance reveals about management’s actions. A favorable demand shock may increase value while saying little about whether a manager worked well.

Use a one-period version of Campus Curb to make the distinction. A manager chooses ordinary or extra effort. Extra effort costs the manager $30 in personal effort; ordinary effort costs zero. Good operating outcomes generate $200 for the business and bad outcomes $50. The good-outcome probability is 80% with extra effort and 40% with ordinary effort. The manager is risk neutral, can accept or reject a contract, and has an outside option worth $40. Outcomes are observable; effort is not.

Contract Expected manager payoff: ordinary effort Expected manager payoff: extra effort Chosen effort
Fixed pay of $40 $40 $40 − $30 = $10 Ordinary
$20 base + $80 if outcome is good $20 + 0.4($80) = $52 $20 + 0.8($80) − $30 = $54 Extra

Both contracts meet the manager’s outside option at the chosen effort. The bonus changes behavior. Expected business receipts are $110 with ordinary effort and $170 with extra effort. Expected compensation is $40 under the fixed contract and $84 under the bonus contract. Expected receipts less compensation rise from $70 to $86 in this example. We compare two feasible contracts; we have not proved that the second is optimal among all contracts.

A bad outcome still occurs 20% of the time under extra effort. An outcome alone does not perfectly reveal an action. An additional measure—such as a maintenance log or peer benchmark—can help when it provides information about effort beyond the measures already used. This is the central intuition of Holmstrom’s informativeness principle (Holmstrom 1979). Adding redundant metrics is not automatically helpful.

4.7 Week 4 drills: information and incentives

Complete after Day 8 and submit Sunday, February 7. These topics are not on Quiz 1. Work individually, check the key, and correct errors. Graded for completion; no generative AI.

  1. Among 100 cases, 25 are high demand and 75 low demand. A report is favorable in 80% of high cases and 20% of low cases. Complete a two-by-two frequency table. What fraction of favorable reports correspond to high demand? Why is the answer not 80%?
  2. Return to the chapter’s 50% prior and the same report accuracy. After a favorable report, the probability of high demand is 80%. Payoffs one year later are $880 or $440. At 10%, calculate expected payoff and value after each report.
  3. At that report date an investor can buy the claim for $700 or decline. Compute the report’s expected decision benefit before its fee. Should the investor purchase information costing $8? What about $11? All costs and values are at that decision date.
  4. For the main text’s manager, compare fixed pay of $40 with $20 plus $80 for a good outcome. Calculate the manager’s net expected payoff under each effort choice and identify the choice. Extra effort costs $30 and raises the good-outcome probability from 40% to 80%.
  5. Classify each: recognizing interest under a known contract; estimating a scooter’s useful life; offering a warranty that costs more for unreliable products; omitting interest because a bill has not arrived. Which can involve private information, and which does not require it?
Drill answers
  1. High: favorable 20, unfavorable 5. Low: favorable 15, unfavorable 60. Posterior high given favorable = 20/35 = 57.14%. The prior is only 25%; a test’s detection rate is not generally the posterior probability.
  2. Favorable: expected payoff $792, value $720. Unfavorable: expected payoff $528, value $480.
  3. Without the report, decline ($600 value < $700 price). With it, buy only after favorable news, giving expected benefit 0.5 × (720 − 700) = $10. An $8 fee leaves $2; an $11 fee leaves −$1, so decline that information offer.
  4. Fixed pay: ordinary $40, extra $10; choose ordinary. Bonus: ordinary $52, extra $54; choose extra. Both chosen payoffs meet the $40 outside option. These are two feasible contracts, not proof of global optimality.
  5. Respectively: routine recognition; estimation; potentially costly signaling; misstatement under the stated facts. Useful-life estimates may incorporate private knowledge; a warranty can reveal type under suitable incentives. Fixed contractual interest recognition need not involve private information.

4.8 Optional extension: a costly signal is not a free improvement

In a stripped-down Spence example (Spence 1973), high-productivity workers produce 100 and low-productivity workers 60. Education \(e\) costs a high type \(e\) and a low type \(2e\) and does not itself raise productivity. Employers pay 100 above threshold \(e^*\) and 60 otherwise.

Separation requires \(100-e^*\ge60\) and \(60\ge100-2e^*\), giving \(20\le e^*\le40\). At 20, high types net 80 and low types net 60; low types are indifferent about mimicking, so the threshold case needs the corresponding tie-breaking behavior.

If the high-type share is \(\lambda\), the no-signal pooling wage is \(60+40\lambda\). Low types lose from separation for every positive \(\lambda\); high types gain only when \(\lambda<0.5\). The signal can sort types while using resources and redistributing pay. It is not automatically a Pareto improvement.

4.9 Optional extension: the LEN model

Suppose output is \(y=e+\varepsilon\) with normally distributed noise of variance \(\sigma^2\), effort cost \(ke^2/2\), a risk-neutral principal, and an agent with exponential utility and absolute risk aversion \(a>0\). Restrict compensation to \(w=b+\beta y\) and choose the fixed payment to satisfy participation. The agent’s certainty equivalent is

\[ CE=b+\beta e-\frac{ke^2}{2}-\frac{a\beta^2\sigma^2}{2}. \]

Optimal effort is \(e=\beta/k\). Within this linear-contract setup the optimal slope is \(\beta^*=1/(1+ak\sigma^2)\). Greater noise or risk aversion weakens incentives because performance pay imposes risk. This is a separate risk-averse extension of the simple classroom contract, not an assumption needed for the main text.
