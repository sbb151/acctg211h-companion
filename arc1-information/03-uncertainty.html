3.1 An uncertain outcome need not be private information

Campus Curb’s founders and investors might agree on the possible outcomes and their probabilities without knowing which will occur. This is uncertainty with symmetric information. It is different from a manager concealing a fact already known to her.

In this chapter, everyone shares the same probability model. Investors are risk neutral: for the payoffs considered here they value an uncertain dollar amount at its expected amount before discounting for time. They can borrow and lend at the stated interest rate, and we retain the other simplifying assumptions from Chapter 2. These assumptions let us introduce uncertainty without simultaneously introducing a pricing model for risk or a credibility problem.

Risk neutrality is a teaching assumption. It does not mean that real investors are indifferent to all risks or that a risky investment should always be discounted at the risk-free rate.

3.2 An expected value is a weighted average

Suppose a claim pays $880 in a high-demand state and $440 in a low-demand state. Each state has probability 0.5. Its expected payoff is

\[ E[X]=0.5(880)+0.5(440)=660. \]

The weights sum to one. They describe our uncertainty, not a promise to receive part of each outcome. The actual receipt will be $880 or $440; it will never be $660 in this example. An expectation can be useful even though it is not a possible realization.

A report that estimates $660 has not necessarily made an error when the outcome is $440. To judge the estimate, ask what information was available when it was made, whether the probability model was appropriate, and whether that model was applied honestly and correctly. A single unfavorable outcome cannot answer all those questions.

3.3 The same enterprise with an uncertain final receipt

Return to the Campus Curb model enterprise. Keep Year 1’s net cash receipt of $660 certain. Year 2’s receipt is now $880 or $440, with equal probability. The annual rate remains 10%, the asset has no value after Year 2, all cash receipts are immediately distributed, and there is no debt. At acquisition, everyone knows this distribution.

At the end of Year 1, just after the first receipt and distribution, the demand state is publicly revealed. After revelation, the appropriate Year 2 receipt is known with certainty. This timing is essential: cash is received in Year 2, but news about that cash arrives in Year 1.

Time Cash and information
0 Buy asset; everyone assigns 50% to each Year 2 state.
End of Year 1 Receive and distribute $660; observe the state.
End of Year 2 Receive and distribute the now-known $880 or $440; asset expires.

At time 0, valuation is

\[ V_0=\frac{660}{1.10}+\frac{0.5(880)+0.5(440)}{(1.10)^2} =1{,}145.45. \]

This is the same initial value as in the certainty case. We held the expected cash flows constant and assumed risk neutrality. Uncertainty alone has not reduced the price under these assumptions.

3.4 Separate the passage of time from news

At the end of Year 1, after paying out the first receipt but just before learning the state, only the Year 2 claim remains. Its expected value is $660/1.10 = $600. There is no retained cash to add. At the same date, immediately after revelation:

\[ V_{1,H}=\frac{880}{1.10}=800, \qquad V_{1,L}=\frac{440}{1.10}=400. \]

The state changes the remaining asset value by +$200 or −$200 relative to the $600 expectation. First-year income includes that surprise:

Year 1 item High state Low state
Expected income from opening value × 10% $114.55 $114.55
Unexpected change in remaining value +200.00 −200.00
Realized economic income $314.55 −$85.45

Verify using distributions plus the change in value:

\[ I_{1,H}=660+800-1{,}145.45=314.55, \]

\[ I_{1,L}=660+400-1{,}145.45=-85.45. \]

A repeatable method
  1. Mark the reporting date and list what is known then. Do not import news that arrives later.
  2. Value only the remaining cash flows, using their probabilities given that information. After the state is revealed here, the appropriate probability is 1 for the revealed outcome.
  3. Add any cash retained by the enterprise to find total ending value. Keep cash paid to owners separate.
  4. Calculate income as distributions plus ending value minus beginning value. To identify news, compare the remaining asset’s values immediately before and after the report at the same date.

Comparing 800 or 400 with the opening 1,145.45 would mix news with a year’s operations and passage of time.

Expected first-year income, assessed at time 0, remains $114.55 because the positive and negative surprises average to zero. The expected value of an unexpected gain or loss is zero relative to the information used to form the expectation; realized gains and losses need not be zero.

Cash arrived, but there is a loss

In the low state, Campus Curb receives $660 and reports an economic loss. The bad news about the remaining asset more than offsets the expected return. Cash received this year does not summarize everything learned about future receipts.

3.5 Accounting for the state revelation

Here is a present-value income statement that separates the two components. We use expected economic depreciation of $545.45 before the state revision, then record the unexpected remeasurement separately.

Year 1 income statement High Low
Net operating receipts $660.00 $660.00
Expected economic depreciation (545.45) (545.45)
Gain/(loss) from new information 200.00 (200.00)
Economic income/(loss) $314.55 ($85.45)
Closing balances and equity reconciliation High Low
Opening equity $1,145.45 $1,145.45
Income/(loss) 314.55 (85.45)
Distribution (660.00) (660.00)
Closing equity $800.00 $400.00
Closing cash 0.00 0.00
Closing delivery asset 800.00 400.00
Closing liabilities 0.00 0.00

The cash-flow statement is identical in both states: $660 from operations, $660 distributed, and no net change in cash. The balance sheet and income statement differ because the same cash receipt accompanies different news.

In Year 2 there is no further surprise. Income is $80 in the high state or $40 in the low state, each 10% of the corresponding opening value. Lifetime income is $394.55 in the high state and −$45.45 in the low state. Check those totals by subtracting the initial investment from lifetime cash distributions. Their average is the certainty case’s $174.55.

These are statements under a stipulated measurement model. Whether GAAP recognizes a particular forecast revision, and where, depends on the relevant accounting requirements. Economic news is not automatically a GAAP entry.

3.6 Expected loss is not the same as a price of risk

Consider three claims paying in two years, discounted at 5% with risk-neutral investors:

Payoff Expected payoff Present value
Certain $1,000 $1,000 $907.03
Equal chances of $500 and $1,500 $1,000 $907.03
90% chance of $1,000; 10% chance of zero $900 $816.33

The third claim is worth $90.70 less than the first because its expected receipt is $100 lower. This is the present value of expected nonpayment. It does not isolate a separate “cost of not knowing.”

Outside our risk-neutral model, investors may demand compensation for bearing priced risk. Which risks affect market prices depends on the economic setting, including how payoffs co-vary with other risks investors bear. Greater variation by itself is not a complete asset-pricing theory.

For this unit, use the supplied risk-neutral assumption. In a later valuation using risk adjustment, explain whether risk is handled in the cash flows, discount rate, or state prices. Do not add a second adjustment for the same risk without examining what the first already includes. We need a consistent model, not a rule that “uncertainty always subtracts value.”

3.7 A useful report can deliver bad news

In the low state, disclosing the news reduces the remaining asset’s value from its prior expectation of $600 to $400. The report has not destroyed a $200 physical resource. It has changed what users know about an already-existing economic situation.

That distinction matters when interpreting evidence. A negative price response does not establish that disclosure was socially harmful. Conversely, an announcement followed by a price increase does not prove that the reporting rule created wealth. Users may simply have learned good news.

The question becomes sharper when users must choose an action. Would a lender offer the same amount after learning the low state? Would a prospective investor buy at the seller’s proposed price? In the next chapter we make the report imperfect and ask how much a decision maker would pay for it.

3.8 Evidence habits: prediction is not proof

This week’s numerical cases are constructed models, not empirical findings. You can verify their arithmetic exactly because their assumptions are specified. A study of real companies faces additional questions: how to measure expectations, when information became available, and what else happened at the same time.

Write two sentences before seeing data: one stating the model’s prediction, and another describing a result that would make you question its application. This habit prepares you for the earnings-announcement evidence in Chapter 6. A model that can “explain” every possible outcome after the fact has not supplied a useful test.

Check timing and retained cash

With delayed revelation, income is $660 + $600 − $1,145.45 = $114.55. There is no state news yet, so do not use the eventual 800 or 400 early.

With high-state revelation and retained cash, assets are $660 cash + $800 remaining claim = $1,460. Distributions are zero, so income is still $314.55. The news component remains +$200; retaining the receipt changes where the owner’s wealth sits, not what was learned about the final payment.

3.10 Week 3 drills: uncertainty and news

Use the stated risk-neutral model and a calculator. Complete after Day 5 and submit before Day 6; check and correct your work. Graded for completion; no generative AI.

  1. A claim pays $154 or $88 in one year, each with probability 0.5. Calculate expected cash and value at 10%. Compare with a certain $121 payment on the same date.
  2. An all-equity enterprise distributes $110 at the end of Year 1 and either $154 or $88 at the end of Year 2, with equal prior probabilities. The rate is 10%. The state is perfectly revealed at the end of Year 1. There is no terminal asset value, no debt, and no further investment. Calculate (a) initial value, (b) Year 1 closing asset and equity in each state, (c) Year 1 income in each state, (d) expected Year 1 income assessed at acquisition, and (e) the expected-return and news components of each outcome.
  3. If Problem 2’s state is revealed only in Year 2 and no other news arrives in Year 1, what are Year 1 closing value and income? Explain what moved to a later period.
  4. At 5% for two years, compare a certain $1,000 with a 90% chance of $1,000 and a 10% chance of zero. How much value is lost? What change in the assumptions accounts for the difference in this calculation?
  5. An unbiased forecasting process sometimes produces estimates above realized cash and sometimes below. Does one low realization establish manipulation? State one piece of evidence you would seek about the original estimate.
Drill answers
  1. Expected cash = $121; value = $110. The certain claim also has value $110 under the supplied assumptions.
    1. 110/1.10 + 121/1.10² = $200. (b) High $140, low $80, for both the remaining asset and equity; closing cash and liabilities are zero. (c) High income = 110 + 140 − 200 = $50; low = 110 + 80 − 200 = −$10. (d) $20. (e) Expected return $20 in both states; news is +$30 or −$30. First-year cash flows are the same in both states.
  3. Closing value $110, income $20. With no Year 1 news, the state-dependent revision is postponed; ultimate operating cash flows are not changed.
  4. Values $907.03 and $816.33; difference $90.70. Expected cash falls from $1,000 to $900. This is a discounted expected-payoff reduction, not an isolated risk premium or value of information.
  5. No. Seek information available at the estimate date, model assumptions, evidence of bias, or consistent application. Do not judge solely with hindsight.

3.11 Optional extension: uncertain probabilities

We have assumed that everyone knows the probabilities. What if founders estimate a 70% chance of high demand and an outside analyst estimates 40%? The disagreement might reflect different evidence, different models, or biased reporting. It need not mean one person is lying.

Compute both valuations, holding everything else fixed. Identify the evidence you would need to distinguish an informed estimate from an optimistic one. This introduces a measurement problem we will return to when discussing faithful representation.
