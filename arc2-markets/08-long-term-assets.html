8.1 What amount helps which decision?

An asset is a present right to an economic benefit; recognition and measurement require additional analysis under the applicable standard. Spending money is not sufficient proof that a valuable asset exists. Nor does the absence of a recognized asset establish that the activity has no future benefit.

Useful reporting needs both relevance and faithful representation, as discussed in Chapter 5. Historical cost does not own faithful representation, and fair value does not own relevance. A purchase invoice can be verifiable while accumulated cost poorly answers today’s resale question. A well-supported fair-value estimate can faithfully represent a relevant current measurement even though it is uncertain. Choosing a basis, estimating its inputs, and checking the evidence are distinct tasks.

A donor deciding whether to fund replacement equipment and a lender estimating collateral proceeds may need different information. Ask what the recorded amount measures and what further evidence the decision needs.

8.2 Equipment: cost and allocation

A community training center pays 12,000 for a machine, 1,000 for delivery, and 2,000 for installation. It also prepays 1,200 for next year’s insurance. Under the case assumptions, equipment cost is 15,000; insurance is a separate prepaid asset, expensed as coverage is consumed. It is not part of equipment cost.

The machine is ready for use immediately and has a four-year useful life and 3,000 residual value. Straight-line depreciation allocates the depreciable amount over the service period:

\[Annual\ depreciation=(15,000-3,000)/4=3,000.\]

Record Dr Depreciation expense 3,000; Cr Accumulated depreciation 3,000. The credit is a contra-asset, leaving original equipment cost visible. Cash does not change at this entry. After two full years the carrying amount is 15,000 − 6,000 = 9,000. Depreciation allocates cost; it does not estimate a new resale price every year.

For partial years, apply the stated convention. An asset ready for use October 1 with full-year depreciation of 6,000 has three months’ depreciation of 1,500 through December 31 under our monthly convention. Acquisition and readiness dates need not coincide in real cases.

8.3 Disposal and impairment answer different questions

If the machine is sold after Year 2 for 8,000, proceeds are 1,000 below its 9,000 carrying amount. Record Dr Cash 8,000; Dr Accumulated depreciation 6,000; Dr Loss 1,000; Cr Equipment 15,000. Remove both original cost and accumulated depreciation. Comparing proceeds with original cost would overstate the loss.

Now consider a separate alternative: the center keeps the machine, and an impairment review is warranted. Treat it as the entire relevant held-and-used asset group for this simplified ASC 360 exercise. Remaining undiscounted cash flows are 8,500 and fair value is 7,000. Apply the supplied two-step rule:

  1. Recoverability screen: carrying amount 9,000 exceeds undiscounted cash flows 8,500.
  2. Loss measurement: write down to fair value 7,000, recognizing a 2,000 loss.

The 500 shortfall at the first step is not the measured loss. If undiscounted cash flows instead were 9,500, this screen would not trigger a loss, even with fair value below carrying amount. This held-and-used rule does not apply universally to goodwill, inventory, or assets held for sale. Real application requires the appropriate grouping and supported cash-flow estimates; the exercise supplies both. SEC correspondence discussing ASC 360’s cash-flow screen.

The board should request evidence on condition, utilization, demand, maintenance, and replacement plans. “Management estimates it” is the start of the investigation, not its end.

8.4 Finite-life intangibles and acquired goodwill

A separately acquired patent costs 60 and has a ten-year useful life, no residual value, and straight-line amortization. Annual expense is 6 and carrying amount after three years is 42. Amortization is the corresponding cost-allocation term for this finite-life intangible.

In a separate simplified business acquisition, an acquirer pays 500 for all of a business whose identifiable assets less assumed liabilities have acquisition-date fair value of 430. With no noncontrolling interest, prior ownership, or other complications, goodwill = 70. This residual is not the total value of all internally developed ideas, people, or reputation.

Under the general U.S. GAAP goodwill model, goodwill is not amortized and is subject to impairment testing. Eligible private companies and not-for-profit entities can elect an alternative including amortization over ten years or a justified shorter life. Entity and election matter. Our goodwill drill asks only the acquisition calculation; no goodwill-impairment calculation is required. FASB ASU 2017-04, FASB ASU 2019-06.

8.5 A capitalization model is sensitive to assumed life

A startup expenses research spending of 90 at the end of Year 1, 120 at the end of Year 2, and 150 at the end of Year 3. An analyst separately constructs a hypothetical cost stock, with straight-line amortization starting in the year after each expenditure. This is neither a proposed universal recognition rule nor observed fair value.

Life assumption Y1 cohort left at end Y3 Y2 cohort left Y3 cohort left Total stock Y3 amortization Y3 income increase versus expensing
3 years 90(1/3) = 30 120(2/3) = 80 150 260 30 + 40 = 70 150 − 70 = 80
5 years 90(3/5) = 54 120(4/5) = 96 150 300 18 + 24 = 42 150 − 42 = 108

Cash spending is identical in both rows. A longer assumed life changes the modeled balance and income without discovering an additional dollar of future benefit. Evidence on product success, customer retention, obsolescence, and useful life is needed before treating a cost stock as decision-useful.

8.6 Market-to-book is not a missing-assets meter

An unrelated company has market equity of 900 and book equity of 300: market-to-book is 3.0. Assume a 100 share repurchase reduces each by exactly 100, with no other change. The ratio becomes 800/200 = 4.0, while the dollar gap stays 600. The exercise created no new intangible asset.

Market equity reflects expectations about future distributions; book equity accumulates recognized transactions and measurements, including distributions. Expected growth, profitability, measurement choices, and distributions all affect their comparison. A high ratio cannot be read directly as the amount of omitted intangible assets. Ratios with very small or negative book equity need particular care.

8.7 What can price associations tell standard setters?

Read only the paired Holthausen–Watts (2001) and Barth–Beaver–Landsman (2001) abstracts, about 15 minutes together, through library/Canvas links. Holthausen and Watts (2001) question the theoretical support for standard-setting inferences drawn from value-relevance studies; Barth, Beaver, and Landsman (2001) argue that this research can inform standard setters. These are methodological arguments. A price association alone does not settle recognition, measurement, implementation cost, or the needs of all report users.

Use the class sensitivity and repurchase cases to identify what additional comparison would help. They are participation work, not another lab or take-home essay. The five packet reading questions are the only required written reading responses.

8.8 Drill Set

Required: 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. Due Sunday March 7, 2027, 11:59 p.m. Eastern, after both class meetings. Graded for completion: attempt the required items yourself, check the key, and correct your work. No generative AI. Optional items: 3–4; optional items do not count toward completion or required assessment.

  1. (Capitalized cost.) A firm buys a machine: invoice price $50,000, freight-in $2,000, installation $3,000, and prepaid insurance for the coming year $1,500. Compute the cost to be capitalized.

  2. (Straight-line depreciation.) The machine costs $55,000, has a $5,000 salvage value, and a five-year useful life. Compute annual depreciation and the book value at the end of year 2.

  3. Optional. (Double-declining-balance.) For the same machine (cost $55,000, salvage $5,000, five-year life), compute depreciation expense for years 1 and 2 under the double-declining-balance method.

  4. Optional. (Units-of-production.) A machine costs $80,000, has an $8,000 salvage value, and is expected to produce 180,000 units over its life. It produces 30,000 units in year 1. Compute year-1 depreciation.

  5. (Partial-year depreciation.) Equipment costing $24,000 (no salvage, four-year life, straight-line) is purchased and ready for use on October 1. Compute depreciation for the first partial calendar year.

  6. (Disposal.) Equipment with a cost of $40,000 and accumulated depreciation of $30,000 is sold for $7,000. Compute the gain or loss and record the journal entry.

  7. (Impairment.) Treat a held-and-used long-lived asset as the entire relevant asset group for this simplified U.S. GAAP case. First compare carrying amount with undiscounted cash flows; if not recoverable, write down to fair value. It has a book value of $120,000, estimated undiscounted future cash flows of $110,000, and a fair value of $95,000. Is the asset impaired, and if so, by how much?

  8. (Goodwill.) In a simplified acquisition of an entire business (no prior ownership or noncontrolling interest), an acquirer pays $500,000 for a target whose identifiable net assets have a fair value of $430,000. Compute goodwill.

  9. (Intangible amortization.) A patent is acquired for $60,000 and has a remaining useful life of ten years, no residual value, and straight-line amortization. Compute annual amortization and the carrying value at the end of year 3.

Drill Set — Self-Check Answers

  1. Capitalized cost. = 50,000 + 2,000 + 3,000 = $55,000. The prepaid insurance ($1,500) is a separate asset initially and becomes expense as coverage is consumed; it is not equipment cost.

  2. Straight-line. Annual depreciation = (55,000 − 5,000) ÷ 5 = $10,000. Book value end of year 2 = 55,000 − 2 × 10,000 = $35,000.

  3. Optional — Double-declining-balance. Rate = 2 ÷ 5 = 40%. Year 1 = 55,000 × 40% = $22,000. Year 2 = (55,000 − 22,000) × 40% = 33,000 × 40% = $13,200. (Do not depreciate below the salvage value.)

  4. Optional — Units-of-production. Rate = (80,000 − 8,000) ÷ 180,000 = $0.40 per unit. Year 1 = 30,000 × 0.40 = $12,000.

  5. Partial year. Full-year depreciation = 24,000 ÷ 4 = $6,000. October 1 to December 31 = 3 months: 6,000 × 3/12 = $1,500.

  6. Disposal. Book value = 40,000 − 30,000 = $10,000. Proceeds $7,000 < $10,000, so loss of $3,000. Entry: Dr Cash $7,000; Dr Accumulated Depreciation $30,000; Dr Loss on Disposal $3,000; Cr Equipment $40,000.

  7. Impairment. Recoverability test: undiscounted cash flows $110,000 < book value $120,000, so the asset is impaired. Impairment loss = book value − fair value = 120,000 − 95,000 = $25,000.

  8. Goodwill. = 500,000 − 430,000 = $70,000.

  9. Patent amortization. Annual = 60,000 ÷ 10 = $6,000. Carrying value end of year 3 = 60,000 − 3 × 6,000 = $42,000.

8.9 Optional extension: other allocation methods

Drill 3 uses double-declining balance: multiply opening carrying amount by twice the straight-line rate, without depreciating below residual value. Drill 4 uses units of production: allocate depreciable cost per estimated lifetime unit, then multiply by actual units. These methods and wider intangible-recognition exceptions are enrichment, excluded from required assessment. Before applying either method to a real entity, investigate the benefit pattern and applicable standard.

Barth, Mary E., William H. Beaver, and Wayne R. Landsman. 2001. “The Relevance of the Value Relevance Literature for Financial Accounting Standard Setting: Another View.” Journal of Accounting and Economics 31 (1–3): 77–104.
Holthausen, Robert W., and Ross L. Watts. 2001. “The Relevance of the Value-Relevance Literature for Financial Accounting Standard Setting.” Journal of Accounting and Economics 31 (1–3): 3–75.
