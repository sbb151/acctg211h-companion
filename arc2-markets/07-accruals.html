7.1 From the December case to an operating-cash reconciliation

In Section 5.5, earning revenue, collecting cash, consuming resources, and paying suppliers could occur at different dates. Those timing differences do not disappear when an organization grows. A customer receivable records a claim from a sale; a payable records an obligation to a supplier. Not every accrual is a forecast of an unobservable amount. Some arise from ordinary contractual timing, while allowances and useful lives require estimates.

Harbor Learning reports net income of 80,000. Its operating balances change as shown below; there are no acquisitions, currency effects, or other reconciling items. The indirect method starts with income and removes the effects that differ from operating cash receipts and payments.

Step CFO adjustment Why? Running total
Net income 80,000 Starting point 80,000
Depreciation +10,000 Expense without current cash payment 90,000
Receivables rise −25,000 Revenue exceeds collections by this change 65,000
Inventory rises −5,000 Purchases exceed inventory cost expensed 60,000
Payables rise +8,000 Some operating purchases remain unpaid 68,000
Contract liabilities rise +12,000 Cash precedes the corresponding service revenue 80,000

Here CFO = 80,000 and net accruals = income − CFO = 0. Zero net accruals do not mean zero accrual components. Several adjustments offset. CFO also does not prove every sale was collected: the increase in receivables says otherwise. Cash collected for next year’s courses brings an obligation to teach them.

For these simplified balances, an increase in a prepaid operating expense subtracts from income in the CFO reconciliation; a decrease adds back. Explain the payment and consumption dates rather than memorizing a sign without a reason.

The cash-flow statement also separates investing cash, such as equipment purchases and sale proceeds, from financing cash, such as borrowing, issuing shares, and paying dividends. In the U.S. GAAP classification used in our drills, interest paid is operating. Noncash transactions require separate attention; recording depreciation is not another cash purchase of the machine.

7.2 Receivables: estimate first, then write off

Suppose gross customer receivables are 10,000 and the supplied estimate of uncollectible amounts is 4%. The required allowance is 400. Because the allowance already has a 100 credit balance, record only 300 additional expense: Dr Bad debt expense 300; Cr Allowance 300. Net receivables are 10,000 − 400 = 9,600. The allowance is a contra-asset, not cash reserved in a bank account.

Next, write off a particular 200 account: Dr Allowance 200; Cr Receivables 200. Gross receivables become 9,800 and the allowance becomes 200, so net receivables remain 9,600. The earlier estimate recognized the loss; this write-off uses the allowance. This is a supplied-percentage teaching case, not a complete expected-credit-loss estimation model.

A lender should ask what supports the estimate: customer aging, collection history, disputes, and current conditions. An estimate deserves evidence; its existence alone is not evidence of manipulation.

7.3 Inventory: allocate a common pool of costs

Start with 10 units at 5 each, buy 10 at 7, and sell 12. There are 20 units costing 120, leaving eight units. Ignore losses and taxes.

Method COGS Ending inventory Check
FIFO 10(5) + 2(7) = 64 8(7) = 56 64 + 56 = 120
Periodic weighted average 12(120/20) = 72 8(120/20) = 48 72 + 48 = 120

FIFO assigns the oldest costs to sales. Periodic weighted average divides total available cost by total available units before allocating it. Cost-flow assumptions need not describe which physical unit a customer received. Cash paid for inventory is unchanged by this cost allocation, although reported COGS and ending inventory differ.

For the FIFO/average-cost inventory examples here, compare cost with net realizable value (estimated selling price less completion, disposal, and transportation costs). If cost is 40,000 and NRV is 37,000, record a 3,000 write-down: Dr Inventory loss or COGS 3,000; Cr Inventory 3,000. The resulting carrying value is 37,000. LIFO and retail-method scope is different and is excluded. FASB ASU 2015-11.

7.4 An identity does not estimate persistence

Define accruals for our classroom case as net income minus reported CFO. Then

\[Income=CFO+Accruals.\]

This is true by construction. A forecast of next year’s income is a different claim requiring evidence or supplied assumptions. Recall Dechow (1994): accrual accounting can help measure performance when cash timing obscures economic activity. That usefulness is compatible with accrual and cash components having different average persistence in a sample.

Two invented firms each have income 100 and average assets 1,000. A has CFO 80 and accruals 20; B has CFO 20 and accruals 80. Use the illustrative rule

\[\widehat{Income}_{t+1}/Assets=0.010+0.85(CFO_t/Assets)+0.75(Accruals_t/Assets).\]

For A: .010 + .85(.08) + .75(.02) = .093. For B: .010 + .85(.02) + .75(.08) = .087. With next year’s denominator held at 1,000, those are forecasts of 93 and 87. Equal slopes of .80 instead produce 90 for both. The identity did not choose the slopes; different assumptions change the forecast difference.

Collecting last year’s receivable creates cash this year without new revenue. That is a timing example. It cannot by itself demonstrate which earnings component predicts future earnings better across continuing businesses.

7.5 Sloan: definitions before coefficients

Sloan (1996) studies 40,679 firm-years over 1962–1991. The paper’s earnings measure is operating income after depreciation, Compustat item 178, described in its continuing-operations framework; it excludes items such as taxes and nonoperating income. Do not substitute ordinary bottom-line net income merely because both measures are called earnings.

The original accrual calculation is the change in noncash current assets, less the change in current liabilities excluding short-term debt and taxes payable, less depreciation/amortization. Its cash component is earnings minus that measure. Variables are scaled by average total assets. This historical balance-sheet construction and today’s reported CFO decomposition need not agree, especially when acquisitions or other nonoperating balance changes intervene.

Table 3 Panel A’s pooled prediction has intercept .011, accrual slope .765, and cash-flow slope .855. Replacing .01 of scaled accruals with .01 of scaled cash flows, holding current earnings fixed, increases fitted future earnings by (.855 − .765)(.01) = .0009 of assets, or 0.09 percentage points. The table reports a test of slope equality; students read that reported comparison, not estimate a regression. Demand changes, measurement differences, or unusual events can make any firm’s outcome differ from the fit. A large accrual is not an individual fraud diagnosis.

7.6 Future returns are a separate question

Sloan’s Table 6 reports year-t+1 size-adjusted portfolio returns of .049 for the lowest accrual decile and −.055 for the highest: a 10.4-percentage-point historical spread. The intermediate series is not strictly decreasing. Returns subtract a size-matched buy-and-hold benchmark and include distributions; measurement begins four months after fiscal year-end. That delay addresses information availability, but a modern test still needs actual release dates.

The paper’s uncertainty calculation uses annual portfolio returns. Ten published decile averages are not ten independent observations for a fresh t-test. Persistence differences alone do not imply unexploited returns: investors may already understand them. Risk benchmarks, costs, availability, and a new sample can alter the conclusion. No modern anomaly profit is promised.

7.8 Drill Set

Required: 1–8. Due Sunday February 28, 2027, 11:59 p.m. Eastern, after both class meetings. Graded for completion: attempt the required items yourself, check the key, and correct your work. No generative AI. All items are required.

  1. (Operating cash flow, indirect method.) Net income is $60,000. For the year: depreciation $10,000; accounts receivable increased $8,000; inventory decreased $3,000; accounts payable increased $5,000. Compute cash flow from operations.

  2. (Classify the cash flows.) Under U.S. GAAP, classify each as operating, investing, or financing: (a) cash paid to purchase equipment; (b) cash received from issuing stock; (c) cash collected from customers; (d) cash paid for dividends; (e) cash paid for interest; (f) cash received from selling a building.

  3. (Direction of the adjustment.) In the indirect method, state whether each is added to or subtracted from net income: (a) increase in accounts receivable; (b) decrease in prepaid expenses; (c) increase in accounts payable; (d) increase in inventory.

  4. (Bad debt expense.) Ending accounts receivable is $200,000; the firm estimates 5% will be uncollectible. The Allowance for Doubtful Accounts has a $3,000 credit balance before adjustment. Compute bad debt expense and record the adjusting entry.

  5. (Write-off.) The firm writes off a specific $1,200 uncollectible account. Record the entry and state the effect on the net realizable value of receivables.

  6. (Inventory — FIFO.) Beginning inventory is 100 units at $11. Purchases during the year are 200 units at $12, then 100 units at $15. The firm sells 250 units. Compute cost of goods sold and ending inventory under FIFO.

  7. (Inventory — weighted average.) Using the same data as Drill 6, compute cost of goods sold and ending inventory under the periodic weighted-average method.

  8. (Lower of cost or net realizable value.) Assume FIFO inventory. Inventory is carried at a cost of $40,000; its net realizable value is $37,000. Record any required adjustment and state the resulting carrying value.

Drill Set — Self-Check Answers

  1. Operating cash flow. CFO = 60,000 + 10,000 (depreciation) − 8,000 (A/R increase) + 3,000 (inventory decrease) + 5,000 (A/P increase) = $70,000.

  2. Classification. (a) investing; (b) financing; (c) operating; (d) financing; (e) operating (U.S. GAAP); (f) investing.

  3. Direction. (a) subtract; (b) add; (c) add; (d) subtract.

  4. Bad debt expense. Desired allowance = 200,000 × 5% = $10,000. Existing credit balance = $3,000, so expense = 10,000 − 3,000 = $7,000. Entry: Dr Bad Debt Expense $7,000; Cr Allowance for Doubtful Accounts $7,000.

  5. Write-off. Dr Allowance for Doubtful Accounts $1,200; Cr Accounts Receivable $1,200. Net realizable value is unchanged (gross receivables and the allowance both fall by $1,200).

  6. FIFO. Units available = 400; sold 250; ending 150. COGS (first in) = 100 × 11 + 150 × 12 = 1,100 + 1,800 = $2,900. Ending inventory = 50 × 12 + 100 × 15 = 600 + 1,500 = $2,100. (Check: 2,900 + 2,100 = $5,000 total cost.)

  7. Weighted average. Total cost = $5,000 ÷ 400 units = $12.50 per unit. COGS = 250 × 12.50 = $3,125. Ending inventory = 150 × 12.50 = $1,875.

  8. Lower of cost or NRV. Write down $3,000 (40,000 − 37,000): Dr Cost of Goods Sold (or Loss on Inventory Writedown) $3,000; Cr Inventory $3,000. Carrying value = $37,000.
