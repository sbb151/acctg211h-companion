10.1 Ownership transactions do not create revenue

A firm issues 10,000 common shares with $1 par value for $15 each. Record Dr Cash 150,000; Cr Common stock 10,000; Cr Additional paid-in capital 140,000. Par value determines this equity presentation; it is not a current market valuation. The cash came from owners, so the transaction increases contributed equity without sales revenue.

The firm separately repurchases 1,000 shares for $18 each. Under the cost method, record Dr Treasury stock 18,000; Cr Cash 18,000. Treasury stock reduces equity; it is not an investment asset. The repurchased shares are no longer outstanding for basic EPS while held in treasury.

If a firm declares a cash dividend of .50 on 200,000 outstanding shares, record Dr Retained earnings (or Dividends) 100,000; Cr Dividends payable 100,000. On payment, debit the liability and credit cash. The dividend distributes equity rather than creating an expense. These entries help explain why Chapter 8’s repurchase can change market-to-book without creating an intangible asset.

10.2 Basic earnings per share needs a time-weighted denominator

For the uncomplicated cases here,

\[Basic\ EPS=\frac{Net\ income-Preferred\ dividends}{Weighted\ average\ common\ shares\ outstanding}.\]

With income 2,400,000, preferred dividends 200,000, and one million weighted-average common shares, EPS is $2.20. A year-end share count alone can misstate the denominator if shares changed during the year.

For example, 800,000 shares outstanding from January 1 plus 240,000 issued July 1 gives 800,000 + 240,000(6/12) = 920,000 weighted-average shares, using the supplied monthly convention. The new shares participate for half the year. Assume no splits, treasury changes, participating securities, or other complications unless a problem supplies them. Diluted EPS is optional.

10.3 Employee service has a cost even without a cash payment

Suppose an equity-classified restricted-stock-unit award has total grant-date fair value of 60,000 and requires three years of service starting January 1. Assume a single cliff-vesting service condition, straight-line expense, no forfeitures, no modification or remeasurement, and no shares issued during this year. Ignore taxes and diluted EPS.

Annual compensation expense is 60,000/3 = 20,000. Record Dr Compensation expense 20,000; Cr Additional paid-in capital 20,000. If income before this expense was 120,000, net income becomes 100,000. With 100,000 common shares outstanding all year and no preferred shares, basic EPS is $1.00.

The entry has no current cash payment, but employees supplied services in exchange for an equity claim. Compensation expense reduces income and retained earnings; the APIC credit records the equity consideration. Both sides matter when explaining the effect on total equity.

A partial-year example uses the service period, not just the calendar-year label. For 12,000 units at a supplied grant-date value of 30, total compensation is 360,000. With a single four-year cliff-vesting service period starting July 1 and straight-line recognition, annual expense is 90,000 and the first six months’ expense is 45,000.

These are deliberately simple equity-classified service awards. Topic 718 distinguishes classification and award conditions; the rule cannot be applied unchanged to liability-classified or every performance/market-condition award. The grant-date measurement and equity credit are discussed in SEC Staff Accounting Bulletin 120. Option pricing and those wider distinctions are extensions, not prerequisites for our calculations.

10.4 A threshold can reward changing the number or the activity

Return to the firm with income of 100,000 after equity compensation. A separate cash bonus of 2,000 is earned if net income before this cash bonus but after equity compensation reaches 105,000. That definition avoids a circular threshold calculation.

The manager proposes reducing an otherwise supported warranty expense estimate by 6,000. Before the cash bonus, income would become 106,000, crossing the threshold. If the bonus is then recognized, final income is 104,000. Changing an expense estimate does not itself create cash. Ask whether new product-quality, repair-cost, or claims evidence justifies the reduction independently of the bonus. An incentive to change an estimate does not establish that any particular revision is false.

As a separate alternative, cancel 6,000 of employee training that otherwise would be expensed and paid this year. Income before the cash bonus again becomes 106,000. This changes a real activity and preserves 6,000 cash before the bonus payment. If the 2,000 bonus is paid now, the net current cash improvement is 4,000. Future service quality or employee retention may suffer. A decision can improve this year’s accounting metric without improving the organization’s long-run outcome.

The comparison extends Chapter 4: incentives affect both effort and choices over what the performance measure records. An employee, board member, or lender needs evidence about the underlying activity as well as the reported number.

10.5 A distribution raises a research question

Before drawing the graph, predict what you would expect near a zero-profit threshold if managers cared about reporting a loss. Make that prediction yourself, without AI. Then sketch these invented counts for adjacent earnings/assets intervals:

Interval Count
[−.02, −.01) 18
[−.01, 0) 10
[0, .01) 24
[.01, .02) 19

The immediate neighbors of zero differ, 10 versus 24. Merging the negative intervals gives 28 and merging the nonnegative ones gives 43, losing the local shape. Bin width, scaling, sample selection, and the underlying economic distribution can affect that picture. These counts were invented for reasoning practice and are not empirical evidence.

Read the Burgstahler–Dichev (1997) abstract, about 20 minutes with the exercise. Burgstahler and Dichev (1997) reports distributional patterns around earnings decreases and losses in historical data. Such patterns motivate questions about reporting behavior; they do not identify which individual firm manipulated earnings. Published article.

Similarly, a correlation between equity pay and misconduct can reflect selection, governance, opportunities, or reverse causality. A causal claim requires a design and its assumptions, not the incentive story alone. Compare what each research design can answer before treating equity compensation as a universal cause of wrongdoing.

10.7 Drill Set

Required: 1–5, 7, 8. Due Sunday March 28, 2027, 11:59 p.m. Eastern, after both class meetings. Graded for completion: attempt the required items yourself, check the key, and correct your work. No generative AI. Optional items: 6; optional items do not count toward completion or required assessment.

  1. (Issue stock above par.) A firm issues 10,000 shares of $1 par common stock for $15 per share. Record the journal entry.

  2. (Treasury stock, cost method.) The firm repurchases 1,000 of its own shares for $18 each. Record the journal entry.

  3. (Cash dividend.) A firm declares a $0.50 per share cash dividend on 200,000 shares outstanding. Record (a) the declaration and (b) the payment.

  4. (Basic EPS.) Net income is $2,400,000, preferred dividends are $200,000, and the weighted-average number of common shares outstanding is 1,000,000. Compute basic earnings per share.

  5. (Weighted-average shares.) A firm has 800,000 shares outstanding on January 1 and issues 240,000 additional shares on July 1. Compute the weighted-average shares outstanding for the year.

  6. Optional. (Diluted EPS, treasury-stock method.) A firm has net income of $1,800,000 and 1,000,000 weighted-average shares. It has 100,000 options outstanding with a $20 exercise price; the average market price is $25. Compute diluted EPS.

  7. (Stock-based compensation expense.) Assume equity classification, a single cliff-vesting service condition, straight-line expense, no forfeitures, no modification or remeasurement, and no taxes. A firm grants options with a total grant-date fair value of $600,000 that vest after three years of service beginning January 1. Compute the annual compensation expense and record one year’s entry.

  8. (RSU expense, partial year.) Assume equity classification, a single cliff-vesting service condition, straight-line expense, no forfeitures, no modification or remeasurement, and no taxes. A firm grants 12,000 restricted stock units on July 1 with a grant-date fair value of $30 each, vesting after four years of service beginning July 1. Compute the compensation expense for the first calendar year (July–December).

Drill Set — Self-Check Answers

  1. Issue above par. Dr Cash $150,000 (10,000 × 15); Cr Common Stock $10,000 (10,000 × 1 par); Cr Additional Paid-in Capital $140,000.

  2. Treasury stock. Dr Treasury Stock $18,000 (1,000 × 18); Cr Cash $18,000.

  3. Cash dividend. (a) Declaration: Dr Retained Earnings (Dividends) $100,000 (200,000 × 0.50); Cr Dividends Payable $100,000. (b) Payment: Dr Dividends Payable $100,000; Cr Cash $100,000.

  4. Basic EPS. = (Net income − preferred dividends) ÷ weighted-average shares = (2,400,000 − 200,000) ÷ 1,000,000 = $2.20.

  5. Weighted-average shares. = 800,000 + 240,000 × (6/12) = 800,000 + 120,000 = 920,000 shares.

  6. Optional — Diluted EPS. Proceeds from exercise = 100,000 × $20 = $2,000,000. Shares repurchased at average price = 2,000,000 ÷ 25 = 80,000. Incremental (net new) shares = 100,000 − 80,000 = 20,000. Diluted shares = 1,000,000 + 20,000 = 1,020,000. Diluted EPS = 1,800,000 ÷ 1,020,000 = $1.76 (down from basic EPS of $1.80).

  7. Option expense. Annual = 600,000 ÷ 3 = $200,000. Entry each year: Dr Compensation Expense $200,000; Cr Additional Paid-in Capital — Stock Options $200,000.

  8. RSU expense. Total grant fair value = 12,000 × $30 = $360,000. Annual expense = 360,000 ÷ 4 = $90,000. First calendar year is a half-year (July–December): 90,000 × 6/12 = $45,000.

10.8 Optional extension: models and estimation

Diluted EPS, option valuation, award taxes, ExecuComp research, and modified Jones estimation are excluded from the required route. For optional Drill 6, assumed option exercise creates proceeds that notionally repurchase shares at the average market price; only incremental shares enter the simplified denominator. Follow the supplied assumptions, not a general rule for all awards.

An adjusted modified-Jones prediction error is not necessarily the residual from the originally estimated regression. When receivables are subtracted from the sales regressor only at prediction, the adjusted error equals the original residual plus the sales coefficient times scaled receivables change. Neither a zero mean nor a zero median is guaranteed. Unusual values can arise from omitted operating features. The legacy code must be audited before any later use; no estimation is assigned here.

Burgstahler, David, and Ilia Dichev. 1997. “Earnings Management to Avoid Earnings Decreases and Losses.” Journal of Accounting and Economics 24 (1): 99–126.
