9.1 An obligation and the incentives around it

A liability is a present obligation to transfer an economic benefit. Borrowing creates a repayment claim, even if management would prefer to use the cash elsewhere. Shareholders can benefit from risky projects or distributions while creditors bear part of the downside. Contracts can limit those choices, require information, and specify when creditors gain additional rights.

An accounting amount is therefore useful both as information and as an input into an agreement. A lender may define “debt” or “earnings” differently from a financial-statement subtotal. Before calculating a covenant ratio, read its numerator, denominator, testing date, threshold, and remedies. A ratio rank among other firms supplies none of those terms.

9.2 Price a bond, then explain its interest expense

A three-year bond promises annual coupons of 600 and principal of 10,000 at the end of Year 3. The market yield at issuance is 8%, with annual compounding and no issuance costs. Use the market rate to discount the promised cash flows:

\[Price=\frac{600}{1.08}+\frac{600}{1.08^2}+\frac{10,600}{1.08^3}=9,484.58.\]

The coupon rate is 600/10,000 = 6%. Since this is below the market yield, investors pay a discount to face value. Using the gross presentation, record Dr Cash 9,484.58; Dr Discount on bonds 515.42; Cr Bonds payable 10,000. The discount reduces the net carrying amount; it is not a separate resource.

The effective-interest method applies the issuance yield to the opening carrying amount:

Year 1 item Calculation Amount
Interest expense 9,484.58 × 8% 758.77
Cash coupon 10,000 × 6% 600.00
Discount amortization Expense − cash 158.77
Ending carrying amount Opening amount + amortization 9,643.35

Record Dr Interest expense 758.77; Cr Cash 600; Cr Discount 158.77. Expense exceeds cash interest because part of the borrowing cost is the increase from discounted proceeds toward the principal ultimately repaid. Round consistently; small rounding differences are acceptable.

At par, a 200,000 bond with a 5% coupon and 5% market yield starts at 200,000, with 10,000 annual cash interest and expense. With a coupon above the issuance market yield, the bond instead sells at a premium and expense is below its coupon. These exercises assume amortized cost and no subsequent modification, default, or fair-value election.

9.3 A lease can create a recognized obligation

For our lease bridge, the lessee owes 1,000 at each of the next two year-ends and discounts at 10%. Assume no prepayments, incentives, initial direct costs, purchase options, or other initial adjustments:

\[Lease\ liability=1,000/1.10+1,000/1.10^2=1,735.54.\]

Record Dr Right-of-use asset 1,735.54; Cr Lease liability 1,735.54. The asset describes a right to use the underlying property for a period; it does not claim ownership of that property. Topic 842 generally places both operating and finance leases on a lessee’s balance sheet, subject to scope and elections such as the short-term exemption. Detailed classification and subsequent expense patterns are optional here. FASB leases overview.

9.4 Contract A and Contract B: the same firm, different compliance

The firm has bank loans 70, bonds 20, lease liabilities 10, EBITDA before permitted adjustments 24, and stock compensation 6, all in millions. EBITDA is an earnings subtotal before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization; adjusted EBITDA depends on which further adjustments a contract permits. Neither is automatically cash available to pay debt.

Contract A tests funded debt divided by trailing-twelve-month adjusted EBITDA at quarter-end, with a maximum of 3.50. Funded debt includes bank loans and bonds but excludes leases. Adjusted EBITDA adds stock compensation to the supplied 24 and permits no other add-backs. There are no waivers or cure rights in this numerical case.

Debt = 70 + 20 = 90; adjusted EBITDA = 24 + 6 = 30; ratio = 3.00. Define upper-limit ratio slack as maximum minus actual: 3.50 − 3.00 = +0.50, so the firm complies. Its debt headroom at fixed EBITDA is 3.50(30) − 90 = 15 million, a different unit from ratio slack.

Contract B includes leases and prohibits the stock-compensation add-back, with the same limit. The ratio is 100/24 = 4.17 and slack is approximately −0.67: a violation under the supplied terms. Identical financial statements have produced different contractual conclusions.

For a minimum interest-coverage test, reverse the subtraction: actual minus minimum. If defined EBIT is 4.8 million, defined interest is 1.2 million, and the minimum is 3.0, actual coverage is 4.0 and slack is +1.0. Define the sign before interpreting it.

9.5 Falling performance and a threshold

Return to Contract A. EBITDA before its permitted adjustment falls to 19 and the add-back remains 6. Adjusted EBITDA is 25; debt remains 90. The ratio becomes 3.60, with slack −0.10.

At fixed adjusted EBITDA, debt must fall to 3.50(25) = 87.5. Repayment of 2.5 million reaches the limit exactly. At fixed debt instead, adjusted EBITDA must reach 90/3.50 = 25.7143 million. Those are separate scenarios, not two changes to combine.

A violation can alter control or bargaining rights without causing immediate liquidation or bankruptcy. Actual outcomes depend on contract terms, timing, cure provisions, waivers, and renegotiation. A maintenance test is checked periodically; an incurrence test applies when a specified action is taken. Do not assume all loans or bonds contain the same tests.

9.6 Evidence about investment and creditor control

Read the Chava–Roberts (2008) abstract, about 15 minutes with the case. Chava and Roberts (2008) study how covenant violations and the associated transfer of control rights affect investment, using a regression-discontinuity design. Publisher abstract.

Weak demand could both reduce earnings and reduce desired investment. Merely comparing highly indebted firms with less indebted firms cannot distinguish that common cause from an effect of creditor control. A comparison close to each firm’s contractual threshold seeks more similar observations on either side. Its causal interpretation still depends on assumptions, including continuity of other determinants and no sorting that defeats comparability at the cutoff. The constructed calculations do not perform that research design, and no universal percentage effect is assigned.

9.7 Why a contract might favor cautious measures

Recognizing losses promptly can give creditors an earlier opportunity to intervene; restricting uncertain gains can reduce premature distributions. This is a proposed contracting explanation for some forms of conservatism, not a universal FASB objective or permission to bias every estimate downward. Neutral reporting and the particular contract’s purpose require separate reasoning. Optional background is Watts (2003) and Smith and Warner (1979); their full papers are not assigned this week.

9.8 Drill Set

Required: 1–6, 8, 9. Due Sunday March 21, 2027, 11:59 p.m. Eastern, after both class meetings. Graded for completion: attempt the required items yourself, check the key, and correct your work. No generative AI. Optional items: 7; optional items do not count toward completion or required assessment.

  1. (Premium or discount?) A bond carries a 6% coupon and is issued when the market rate is 8%. Will it sell at a premium, a discount, or par? Over the bond’s life, will interest expense be greater than, less than, or equal to the cash coupon?

  2. (Issue price.) Compute the issue price of a three-year, $100,000 bond with a 6% annual coupon when the market rate is 8%. Use annual compounding.

  3. (Effective-interest amortization.) The bond in Drill 2 is issued at $94,846. Under the effective-interest method, compute year-1 interest expense, the discount amortized, and the carrying value at the end of year 1.

  4. (Issuance entry.) Record the journal entry to issue the bond in Drill 2 (use the gross method, with a separate discount account).

  5. (Bond at par.) A $200,000 bond with a 5% annual coupon is issued at par. Record (a) the issuance entry and (b) the first annual interest-payment entry.

  6. (Lease liability.) Assume no prepayments, incentives, or other initial adjustments. A four-year lease requires payments of $10,000 at the end of each year; the discount rate is 6%. Compute the initial lease liability (equal to the right-of-use asset).

  7. Optional. (Lease classification.) Consider each feature in isolation under ASC 842. State whether it points to a finance-lease criterion; a feature alone may be insufficient to classify the entire lease: (a) ownership transfers to the lessee at the end of the term; (b) the lease term is nine months with no purchase option; (c) the term covers 90% of the asset’s economic life; (d) the present value of payments is 97% of the asset’s fair value.

  8. (Interest-coverage covenant.) A firm has EBIT of $4,800,000 and interest expense of $1,200,000. The contract defines coverage as the supplied EBIT divided by the supplied interest expense and requires coverage of at least 3.0×. Compute the ratio and the covenant slack.

  9. (Debt-to-EBITDA covenant.) Use these amounts as the contractually defined numerator and denominator, with no other adjustments. A firm has total debt of $90 million and EBITDA of $30 million. A covenant caps debt-to-EBITDA at 3.5×. Compute the ratio and state whether the covenant is satisfied.

Drill Set — Self-Check Answers

  1. Premium or discount? Discount — the coupon (6%) is below the market rate (8%), so investors will pay less than face. Interest expense will be greater than the cash coupon, because expense is the carrying amount times the 8% market rate.

  2. Issue price. Present value at 8%: coupons = 6,000 × annuity factor (2.577097) = $15,463; face = 100,000 × PV factor (0.793832) = $79,383. Issue price ≈ $94,846 (a $5,154 discount).

  3. Effective-interest amortization. Year-1 interest expense = 94,846 × 8% = $7,588. Cash coupon = $6,000, so discount amortized = 7,588 − 6,000 = $1,588. Carrying value end of year 1 = 94,846 + 1,588 = $96,434.

  4. Issuance entry (gross method). Dr Cash $94,846; Dr Discount on Bonds Payable $5,154; Cr Bonds Payable $100,000.

  5. Bond at par. (a) Dr Cash $200,000; Cr Bonds Payable $200,000. (b) Dr Interest Expense $10,000 (200,000 × 5%); Cr Cash $10,000.

  6. Lease liability. PV of $10,000 for four years at 6% = 10,000 × annuity factor (3.4651) = $34,651.

  7. Optional — Lease classification. (a) ownership transfer points to finance classification; (b) a nine-month term alone does not establish or exclude finance classification, and the full contract and any short-term election matter; (c) 90% points to a major part of economic life; (d) 97% points to substantially all of fair value. These percentages illustrate judgments, not mandatory bright-line thresholds. Other relevant terms still need review.

  8. Interest coverage. Ratio = 4,800,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 4.0×. Slack = 4.0 − 3.0 = 1.0× (in compliance).

  9. Debt-to-EBITDA. Ratio = 90 ÷ 30 = 3.0×. Since 3.0× ≤ 3.5×, the covenant is satisfied (slack of 0.5×).

9.9 Optional extension: beyond the supplied contract

Lease classification requires the full set of relevant terms; a short term alone does not rule out every finance-lease criterion. Pension/OPEB accounting, contingent-liability recognition, and detailed lease schedules are outside required assessment. A later real-contract investigation must supply the agreement, amendments, actual testing dates, and definitions. Cross-sectional financial-ratio ranks cannot be relabeled contractual slack.
