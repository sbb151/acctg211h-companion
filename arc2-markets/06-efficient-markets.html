6.1 An increase can still disappoint

Elm Devices announces earnings per share (EPS) of $1.20. Last year’s comparable EPS was $1.00, but a forecast available before the announcement was $1.40. Earnings increased by $0.20 and fell short of the forecast by $0.20. These comparisons answer different questions. News is the difference between what was learned and what was expected; it is not simply the sign of profit or its change from last year.

The forecast and actual number must use the same accounting definition and share basis. A forecast of adjusted EPS cannot automatically be compared with GAAP EPS. Nor does a forecast necessarily describe every investor’s expectations. In this exercise it is a supplied benchmark, not an observed consensus we have independently verified.

A job candidate might investigate whether disappointing news changes expected hiring or the firm’s ability to pay wages. A lender might investigate cash available for repayment. An investor might revise expected distributions. A report can be useful even when its news is unwelcome, as in Chapter 4.

6.2 Revenue is earned as obligations are satisfied

Before interpreting earnings news, understand what enters the number. In this simplified application of Topic 606, identify the customer contract and distinct performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it using relative standalone selling prices, and recognize revenue when or as each obligation is satisfied. These steps organize the analysis; contractual details can change its result. FASB revenue-recognition overview.

A vendor collects $900 for a product delivered today and support over the next twelve months. The standalone prices are $800 and $400. Assume distinct obligations and evenly delivered support; ignore costs and taxes.

Calculation Result
Total standalone prices 800 + 400 = 1,200
Product allocation 900 × 800/1,200 = 600
Support allocation 900 × 400/1,200 = 300
Support revenue each month 300/12 = 25

At delivery: Dr Cash 900; Cr Revenue 600; Cr Contract liability 300. A credit to the liability records the remaining service obligation. After one month: Dr Contract liability 25; Cr Service revenue 25. There is no second cash receipt. Predicting cash correctly while recognizing the entire 900 as current revenue would still overpredict earnings.

For a separate, simplified return-right example, goods sell for 50,000 and expected refunds are 4% of that amount. Assume the estimate meets the applicable revenue constraints. Recognize revenue of 48,000 and a refund liability of 2,000. This isolates the revenue side; inventory recovery and cost entries require additional facts and are outside this drill.

6.3 Measure a return relative to a benchmark

A stock’s return includes influences shared with other stocks. A market-adjusted abnormal return subtracts the same day’s market return:

\[AR_t=R_{stock,t}-R_{market,t},\qquad CAR[a,b]=\sum_{t=a}^{b}AR_t.\]

CAR is a sum over a specified event window. It is not a price level. Here day 0 is the trading day on which the release becomes public before trading opens; each return is close-to-close for that day. An after-close release would first enter the following trading day’s close-to-close window.

Day Elm return Market return AR
−1 0.4% 0.1% 0.3%
0 −2.0% −0.5% −1.5%
+1 0.3% 0.2% 0.1%

The three-day CAR is −1.1%. The release also reduced sales guidance. Even with correct arithmetic, this window cannot identify the EPS-only effect: investors received a bundle of news. A negative CAR does not establish impending bankruptcy or job losses.

A market model supplies a different benchmark, \(\alpha+\beta R_{market}\). You need not estimate one. With supplied \(\alpha=0\), \(\beta=1.2\), a market return of 1.5%, and a stock return of 3.2%, the predicted return is 1.8% and AR is 1.4%. Beta and the benchmark choice matter. Keep all returns in compatible units.

Do not compound \(1+AR\) and call the result CAR. A buy-and-hold abnormal return is yet another measure: compounded stock performance minus compounded benchmark performance.

6.4 Efficiency is a benchmark, with qualifications

Fama (1970) distinguishes information sets: past prices in weak-form efficiency, public information in semi-strong efficiency, and public plus private information in strong-form efficiency. The taxonomy is background explained here; the full paper is optional.

The semi-strong benchmark asks whether publicly available information permits systematic abnormal profits after allowing for risk and trading costs. It does not predict that prices never move or that all investors earn the same realized return. A rapid response to genuinely new public information is compatible with efficiency.

An empirical test also needs a model of the return required for risk. An apparent abnormal return might reflect slow information processing, an incomplete risk benchmark, or trading frictions. This joint-hypothesis problem limits what a pattern can establish. One successful trade does not refute efficiency; an announcement response does not prove it.

6.5 Ball and Brown: what the historical picture measures

Ball and Brown (1968) examine 261 firms over fiscal years 1957–1965. Their regression news benchmark relates a firm’s annual income change to the market-wide annual income change using firm-specific estimated relationships. It is not an analyst forecast or a cross-sectional median split. Their alternative naive benchmark uses the prior year’s income, but our lab uses the regression-model net-income groups. Estimation details are optional; the contemporaneous market-income input and eventual earnings realization mean this is not a forecast portfolio known a year in advance.

Their return analysis uses monthly, not daily, observations. The abnormal-performance index (API) averages firm-level compounded monthly residual-return paths, starting at 1 before month −11. It is a different statistic from our summed three-day CAR. Month 0 is the annual-announcement month.

Selected Table 5 observation Positive forecast error API Negative forecast error API
Month −1 1.060 0.914
Month 0 1.071 0.907

The group gap is 0.146 before month 0 and 0.164 at month 0: an increase of 0.018 index units. Much of the separation appears earlier. Other information can reach investors before the annual report, while some news remains at announcement. Neither “the report says nothing” nor “the whole year’s separation was caused by the announcement” follows.

The sample excludes many firms, and its long window contains other events. Classification uses annual earnings information unavailable throughout the preceding year. A graph formed with hindsight can illuminate an association without supplying an implementable trading strategy.

6.7 Drill Set

Required: 1–6. Due Sunday February 21, 2027, 11:59 p.m. Eastern, after both class meetings. Graded for completion: attempt the required items yourself, check the key, and correct your work. No generative AI. All items are required.

  1. (Allocate the transaction price.) A firm sells a bundle for $900: a product with a standalone selling price of $800 and a one-year service with a standalone selling price of $400. Allocate the $900 to the two performance obligations using relative standalone selling prices.

  2. (Recognize over time.) The service from Drill 1 is delivered evenly over twelve months. How much service revenue is recognized in the first two months?

  3. (Sale with a contract liability.) On January 1 the firm receives the $900 cash for the bundle in Drill 1 and delivers the product immediately; the service has not yet been provided. Record (a) the January 1 entry and (b) the January 31 entry for one month of service.

  4. (Right of return.) A firm ships goods on December 28 for $50,000; control transfers on shipment and the firm reliably estimates that 4% will be returned. How much revenue is recognized in December, and what refund liability is recorded?

  5. (Abnormal return.) A stock returns +3.2% on its earnings-announcement day. Its market-model parameters are \(\alpha \approx 0\) and \(\beta = 1.2\), and the market returned +1.5% that day. Compute the abnormal return.

  6. (Cumulative abnormal return.) Over a three-day event window a stock’s daily abnormal returns are +0.6%, −0.2%, and +1.1%. Compute the cumulative abnormal return (CAR).

Drill Set — Self-Check Answers

  1. Allocate. Total standalone value = 800 + 400 = $1,200. Product = 900 × (800/1,200) = $600. Service = 900 × (400/1,200) = $300.

  2. Over time. Service revenue = 300 × (2/12) = $50.

  3. Contract liability.

      1. January 1: Dr Cash $900; Cr Sales Revenue $600 (product delivered); Cr Deferred (Contract) Revenue $300.
      1. January 31: Dr Deferred Revenue $25 (= 300 ÷ 12); Cr Service Revenue $25.

  4. Right of return. Revenue recognized = 50,000 × 96% = $48,000; Refund liability = 50,000 × 4% = $2,000.

  5. Abnormal return. Expected return = \(\alpha + \beta R_m\) = 0 + 1.2 × 1.5% = 1.8%. AR = 3.2% − 1.8% = +1.4%.

  6. CAR. CAR = 0.6% − 0.2% + 1.1% = +1.5%.
