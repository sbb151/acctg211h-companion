11.1 Who should decide?

Imagine a service network for Tesla or Rivian with three locations. This is a constructed organizational case, not a description of either company’s actual delegation policy. A local manager knows service queues, staffing, and parts delays; headquarters sees capital needs and purchasing opportunities across locations. Who should set service hours, approve suppliers, and authorize a new facility?

Hayek (1945) emphasizes knowledge of particular circumstances held by people close to the activity. Moving every decision to the center can discard that information or require costly communication. Delegation lets the local manager act on it. Delegation also gives someone with their own preferences discretion over the organization’s resources. A manager might prefer convenient hours, an oversized staff or a project that expands their role.

The choice depends on both information and incentives. Let a local manager adapt opening hours within a budget, coordinate repair-quality procedures across locations, and have the center evaluate a large capital investment using local forecasts and independent evidence. No single organizational level must own every decision. The same reasoning applies to charities, hospital departments and university colleges.

Internal accounting helps the manager choose and helps others evaluate that choice. These uses can conflict. A cost allocated for performance reporting need not be avoidable when making a particular decision.

11.2 Match accountability to decision rights

A responsibility center is a unit with a manager, a defined set of decisions and a performance report.

Center Responsibility in the specified role Example
Cost Costs of providing a defined service Payroll office with a service-quality requirement
Revenue Sales, within delegated pricing/customer rules Sales territory without production authority
Profit Revenue and operating costs A location choosing its product mix and staffing
Investment Profit and capital deployed A division with operating-investment authority

These labels describe delegated authority, not an immutable property of an industry. Evaluate controllable actions, recognizing that managers influence some outcomes jointly. A sales manager should not gain by selling products the firm loses money supplying. A cost center should not gain by cutting essential service quality.

11.3 Cost behavior within a stated range

A variable cost changes in total with activity. A fixed cost remains unchanged in total over the specified activity range and horizon. Fixed cost per unit falls as volume rises; that does not make total cost variable. A mixed cost contains both components. Room rental can be fixed for one semester and avoidable when the contract expires. An additional supervisor can create a step when activity exceeds capacity.

For our constructed examples assume one product, constant price and variable cost per unit, unchanged total fixed cost, adequate capacity, and production equal to sales. Test these assumptions before applying the model to a real organization.

Let price be P, variable cost per unit V, quantity Q and fixed cost F:

\[CM\ per\ unit=P-V;\qquad Operating\ income=Q(P-V)-F.\]

Contribution margin pays for fixed costs and then contributes to operating income. It is not gross profit: financial-statement cost of goods sold need not equal variable cost.

11.4 A complete contribution-margin example

A constructed ticket business charges $40, incurs $25 variable cost per attendee and has $120,000 fixed cost. At 11,000 attendees:

Item Calculation Dollars
Sales 11,000 × 40 440,000
Variable cost 11,000 × 25 (275,000)
Contribution margin 11,000 × 15 165,000
Fixed cost Given (120,000)
Operating income 165,000 − 120,000 45,000

The contribution-margin ratio is 15/40 = 37.5%. Break-even units are F/(P−V) = 120,000/15 = 8,000. Break-even sales dollars are F/CM ratio = 120,000/.375 = 320,000. To earn target operating income T, required units are (F+T)/(P−V). For T = 45,000, the result is 11,000. Round up when only whole units can be sold.

Degree of operating leverage is total contribution margin divided by operating income: 165,000/45,000 = 3.667. A 10% volume increase, with price and cost assumptions unchanged, adds 16,500 of contribution margin and income. Income becomes 61,500, a 36.667% rise. This multiplication works within the same linear cost model. At break-even the denominator is zero; near break-even the ratio can be very large. A negative starting income makes percentage interpretations awkward, so show dollars too.

11.5 Comparing two ways to run a festival

Our in-class Summit Sound Festival retains the constructed numbers from the earlier activity, but all required work uses a calculator.

Input Automated stage A Modular rental B
Ticket price $125 $125
Variable cost per attendee $15 $45
Fixed cost $450,000 $150,000

At 12,000 attendees, A earns 12,000(110)−450,000 = 870,000; B earns 12,000(80)−150,000 = 810,000. Their profits are equal when 110Q−450,000 = 80Q−150,000, or Q = 10,000, yielding profit of 650,000 each. Above 10,000, A’s larger contribution margin wins; below it, B’s lower fixed commitment helps. Break-even attendance, rounded up, is 4,091 for A and 1,875 for B. The indifference point is not a break-even point.

At 9,600 attendees, income is 606,000 and 618,000. The 20% attendance decline reduces income by 30.345% and 23.704%, respectively. Operating leverage depends on starting volume and profit as well as cost structure. Choosing B might buy flexibility but sacrifices contribution margin when attendance is high. A forecast and the decision-maker’s objectives are needed for a recommendation.

11.6 Allocation changes the reported comparison

Two divisions earn contribution after their own traceable fixed costs of $8 million and $7 million. They share $4 million of corporate overhead. Consumer generates 60% of revenue but occupies only 25% of floor space.

Allocation Consumer overhead Industrial overhead Consumer profit Industrial profit
Revenue shares 60%/40% 2.4 1.6 5.6 5.4
Floor-space shares 25%/75% 1.0 3.0 7.0 4.0

All figures are millions. Total profit remains 11 million. The base changes each manager’s reported performance without changing total resources used. A plausible base relates to resource consumption, but floor space may explain occupancy cost better than legal support. Use separate bases if their information benefits justify the effort.

If a division appears unprofitable after allocation, closing it creates value only if the revenue and avoidable costs lost support that conclusion. Allocated corporate rent that continues after closure is not saved. Ask what changes, over what period, and who controls it.

11.7 What cost information can establish

Rivian’s fiscal 2025 Form 10-K reports revenue of $5.387 billion, gross profit of $0.144 billion, and an operating loss of $3.585 billion. Its 2024 comparative figures are revenue $4.970 billion, gross loss $1.200 billion, and operating loss $4.689 billion. Gross profit improves by $1.344 billion, but it is neither operating profit nor contribution margin. Rivian 2025 Form 10-K, printed p. 75.

Use the festival model to learn the calculation, then ask which assumptions a Rivian or Tesla application needs. The reported totals do not identify variable cost per vehicle or fixed cost. Changes in mix, prices, production and other circumstances can affect the comparison. Do not label assumed costs as observed 10-K data or interpret the revenue/profit association as a causal estimate of operating leverage.

Self-check. Why does fixed cost per attendee fall from 15 at break-even to about 10.91 at 11,000 attendees? The same 120,000 commitment is divided among more attendees. The decrease in the average does not itself save cash.

11.8 Drill Set

Required: 2–5 and 7–8. Optional: 1 and 6. Complete the required items after instruction by April 4, 2027, 11:59 p.m. Eastern. Work individually by hand, check the key, and correct errors. Graded for completion; optional items are excluded from quizzes and the final.

  1. (High-low method.) At 10,000 units of activity total cost is $80,000; at 6,000 units total cost is $56,000. Compute (a) the variable cost per unit and (b) total fixed cost.

  2. (Contribution margin.) A product sells for $40 and has a variable cost of $25 per unit. Compute (a) the unit contribution margin and (b) the contribution-margin ratio.

  3. (Break-even in units.) Fixed costs are $120,000 and the unit contribution margin is $15. Compute the break-even point in units.

  4. (Break-even in dollars.) Fixed costs are $120,000 and the contribution-margin ratio is 37.5%. Compute break-even sales dollars.

  5. (Target profit.) Using a unit contribution margin of $15 and fixed costs of $120,000, compute the number of units required to earn a target operating profit of $45,000.

  6. (Margin of safety.) Budgeted sales are $500,000 and break-even sales are $320,000. Compute the margin of safety in dollars and as a percentage of sales.

  7. (Contribution-margin income statement.) A firm sells 11,000 units at $40 each; variable cost is $25 per unit; fixed costs are $120,000. Prepare a contribution-margin income statement (sales, variable costs, contribution margin, fixed costs, operating income).

  8. (Operating leverage.) Using the contribution margin ($165,000) and operating income ($45,000) from Drill 7, compute the degree of operating leverage, and predict the percentage change in operating income if sales rise 10%.

Drill Set — Self-Check Answers

Required answers: 2–5 and 7–8. Other answers support optional practice.

  1. High-low. Variable cost per unit = (80,000 − 56,000) ÷ (10,000 − 6,000) = 24,000 ÷ 4,000 = $6.00. Fixed cost = 80,000 − 6.00 × 10,000 = $20,000 (check at low point: 56,000 − 6.00 × 6,000 = 20,000).

  2. Contribution margin. Unit CM = 40 − 25 = $15. CM ratio = 15 ÷ 40 = 37.5%.

  3. Break-even units. = 120,000 ÷ 15 = 8,000 units.

  4. Break-even dollars. = 120,000 ÷ 0.375 = $320,000.

  5. Target profit. = (120,000 + 45,000) ÷ 15 = 165,000 ÷ 15 = 11,000 units.

  6. Margin of safety. = 500,000 − 320,000 = $180,000; as a percentage = 180,000 ÷ 500,000 = 36%.

  7. Contribution-margin income statement.

    Income statement line Amount (dollars)
    Sales (11,000 × $40) 440,000
    Variable costs (11,000 × $25) (275,000)
    Contribution margin 165,000
    Fixed costs (120,000)
    Operating income 45,000

  8. Operating leverage. Degree of operating leverage = contribution margin ÷ operating income = 165,000 ÷ 45,000 = 3.67. A 10% rise in sales raises operating income by 3.67 × 10% ≈ 36.7%.

11.9 Optional extension: transfer prices and richer costs

A transfer price assigns revenue and cost to divisions trading internally. With spare capacity, no displaced sale and incremental cost $40, a transfer can be worthwhile when the receiving division’s incremental benefit exceeds $40. If supplying internally displaces an outside sale, include the lost contribution as opportunity cost. A market price is not automatically the marginal cost of an internal transfer. Variable-cost pricing can give the buyer a useful marginal signal while leaving the seller needing another way to recover capacity costs; full-cost pricing can affect sourcing incentives. Internal evaluation and tax transfer-pricing compliance are separate design questions.

High-low estimation, margin of safety, activity-based costing and cost stickiness are optional extensions. They are not required for Quiz 4 or the final. The next chapter asks how a report changes choices even when the arithmetic is correct.
