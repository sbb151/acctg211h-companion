13.1 Start with incremental cash flows

A machine may make a department’s reported profit larger while destroying value. A worthwhile investment may lower its short-term ROI. Evaluate the cash flows that change when the organization accepts the project, including the timing of costs, benefits and any recovery at the end.

For a constructed example, pay 100 now to receive 60 at the end of each of the next two years. The supplied discount rate is 10%; these are incremental after-tax cash flows with no other outlays or terminal proceeds.

\[NPV=-100+\frac{60}{1.10}+\frac{60}{1.10^2}=4.132.\]

The 100 is paid now and is not discounted. The first 60 has PV 54.545; the second has PV 49.587. Positive NPV means the project adds value under these cash-flow and discount-rate assumptions. Do not subtract depreciation as a second cash outlay when the equipment payment is already included. If taxes matter, their cash effects must be included; this course supplies them or explicitly ignores taxes.

A sunk cost paid before today’s decision is not changed by that decision. The cash from selling an existing asset instead of using it is an opportunity cost. Working capital tied up initially and recovered at the end belongs in the timeline. State what is included, not merely a formula.

13.3 Flexibility belongs inside the valuation

A rigid plan and a contingent plan must use the same states and payoffs if their difference is to measure flexibility. Consider a two-stage project, amounts in millions. Assume risk neutrality, a 10% discount rate, and no cash flows beyond those specified.

Pay 4 now to learn at time 1 whether demand is strong or weak, each with probability .5. Commercialization at time 1 costs 8. Its cash payoff at time 2 is 16 in the strong state and 4 in the weak state.

At time 1, commercialization NPV is −8+16/1.10 = 6.545 if strong and −8+4/1.10 = −4.364 if weak. A flexible manager commercializes only if strong.

Policy after paying for research Value at time 0
Must commercialize in either state −4 − 8/1.10 + [.5(16)+.5(4)]/1.10² = −3.008
May decline commercialization after seeing demand −4 + .5[−8+16/1.10]/1.10 = −1.025

The option to decline adds 1.983 million, the present value of avoiding the weak-state loss. Both policies still have negative initial NPV, so the firm should decline the research investment if these are the only benefits. Valuable flexibility does not guarantee a valuable overall project.

NPV is capable of valuing this contingent policy. A calculation that already conditions the follow-on cost on success already includes that flexibility; do not add it again. Uncertainty alone is not option value. Value comes from a useful future choice informed by what is learned.

13.4 Information changes the allocation problem

The center cannot evaluate projects well if managers have private information and gain from obtaining capital regardless of value. Antle and Eppen (1985) models a relationship among private information, organizational slack, rationing and cutoff rates above market rates. This establishes possibilities under a model’s assumptions, not a universal instruction to cut every request by 20%. Publisher abstract.

The in-class allocation game uses indivisible one-period projects, a fixed budget and privately known terminal cash. Terminal cash includes recovery of the investment. A project’s value is:

\[NPV=-Investment+\frac{Terminal\ cash}{1.10}.\]

For investment 40 and terminal cash 48, NPV is 3.636, not 48−.10(40). If accounting profit were 8 and average capital 40 on a compatible one-period basis, RI would be 4; discounted RI is 4/1.10 = 3.636. These quantities have different timing and names.

Managers in the game earn a bonus for funded capital; headquarters scores total NPV. The bonus therefore creates a reason to request funds even for a negative-NPV project. Headquarters must consider evidence and its verification cost. In a second round, one independently checked claim provides information before allocation. Compare outcomes with the feasible optimum, but do not interpret a few classroom rounds as an equilibrium estimate or a causal test of a general theory.

Self-check. With budget 60, indivisible project A costs 40 and has NPV 8; B costs 30 with NPV 7; C costs 30 with NPV 7. Fund B+C, NPV 14, rather than A alone. Ranking individual projects by absolute NPV misses the combination constraint.

13.5 Drill Set

Required: 1–5. Optional: 6–8. Complete the required items after instruction by April 18, 2027, 11:59 p.m. Eastern. Work individually by hand, check the key, and correct errors. Graded for completion; optional items are excluded from quizzes and the final.

  1. (Payback, even cash flows.) A project costs $240,000 and generates $60,000 per year. Compute the payback period assuming cash arrives uniformly within each year.

  2. (Payback, uneven cash flows.) A project costs $100,000 and generates $30,000, $40,000, $50,000, and $60,000 in years 1 through 4. Compute the payback period assuming cash arrives uniformly within each year.

  3. (Present value of a single sum.) A project returns a single cash flow of $50,000 at the end of year 3. At a 10% discount rate, compute its present value.

  4. (NPV, annuity.) A project costs $75,000 and generates $25,000 per year for four years. The discount rate is 10%. Compute the net present value.

  5. (NPV, uneven cash flows.) A project costs $60,000 and generates $20,000, $30,000, and $25,000 in years 1 through 3. The discount rate is 8%. Compute the net present value.

  6. (Optional: IRR decision rule.) Assume an initial outflow followed only by positive inflows. A project has an internal rate of return of 9% and the firm’s cost of capital is 11%. Should the project be accepted? At the cost of capital, is the project’s NPV positive, negative, or zero?

  7. (IRR, even cash flows.) A project costs $79,247 and returns $25,000 per year for four years. Compute the annuity factor (cost ÷ annual cash flow), then identify the IRR given that the four-year annuity factor equals 3.1699 at 10%.

  8. (Profitability index.) A project requires an initial investment of $50,000 and the present value of its future cash flows is $58,000. Compute the profitability index and state the decision.

Supplied factors: 10%, year 3: 0.751315; 10%, four-year annuity: 3.169865. At 8%, years 1–3: 0.925926, 0.857339, 0.793832. Round final dollars; small factor-rounding differences are acceptable.

Drill Set — Self-Check Answers

Required answers: 1–5. Other answers support optional practice.

  1. Payback, even. = 240,000 ÷ 60,000 = 4.0 years.

  2. Payback, uneven. Cumulative cash flow: year 1 = 30,000; year 2 = 70,000; year 3 = 120,000. The $100,000 cost is recovered during year 3: after year 2, $30,000 remains, and year 3 brings $50,000, so 30,000 ÷ 50,000 = 0.6. Payback = 2.6 years under uniform intra-year receipts; if receipts arrive only at year-end, recovery occurs at the end of year 3.

  3. PV of a single sum. = 50,000 ÷ 1.10³ = 50,000 ÷ 1.331 = $37,566.

  4. NPV, annuity. Annuity factor (10%, 4 years) = 3.1699. PV of inflows = 25,000 × 3.1699 = $79,247. NPV = 79,247 − 75,000 = $4,247.

  5. NPV, uneven. PVs at 8%: year 1 = 20,000 ÷ 1.08 = 18,519; year 2 = 30,000 ÷ 1.08² = 25,720; year 3 = 25,000 ÷ 1.08³ = 19,846. Sum ≈ 64,084. NPV = 64,084 − 60,000 = $4,084.

  6. IRR rule. For the conventional cash-flow pattern specified, reject — the IRR (9%) is below the cost of capital (11%). At an 11% discount rate the NPV is negative (NPV equals zero only at the 9% IRR, and falls as the discount rate rises).

  7. IRR, even. Annuity factor = 79,247 ÷ 25,000 = 3.1699. Because that equals the four-year annuity factor at 10%, the IRR = 10%.

  8. Profitability index. = PV of future cash flows ÷ initial investment = 58,000 ÷ 50,000 = 1.16. Since PI > 1.0, accept the project.

13.6 Optional extension: IRR and profitability index

IRR is a discount rate at which NPV equals zero. With one initial outflow followed only by positive inflows, a unique positive IRR, when it exists, can be compared with the compatible required return. The extended example’s IRR is 17.178948%. For mutually exclusive projects, dollar NPV at the appropriate rate governs value; a smaller project can have a higher IRR but less NPV.

Multiple sign changes permit multiple IRRs but do not ensure them. Cash flows −100, 230, −132 have IRRs 10% and 20%. In contrast, −100, 400, −400 has NPV = −100(r−1)²/(1+r)² and only one distinct IRR, 100%, a repeated root. Use the NPV at the required rate to avoid interpreting an arbitrary solver root as a decision rule.

Profitability index is PV of future inflows divided by initial cost, or 1+NPV/cost for this simple structure. Under special divisible-project assumptions it can help allocate a limited budget. With indivisible projects, check feasible combinations. Formal option pricing, IRR solving, PI optimization, influence-cost models and the empirical diversification literature are optional, outside the final’s required scope.
